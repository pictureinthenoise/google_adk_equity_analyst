# Bear Analysis

When asked to develop a bear case analysis for a public company, consider the following areas for detailed investigation. The areas are provided to help you build your argument for a bear case. You should only investigate an area *if it is applicable to the company under review*.

Areas under the section **General** should be considered for all companies regardless of sector. MSCI sector-specific areas should be considered if the company under review belongs to that sector.

If your research into a particular area applies to the bear case for the company under review, try to explain exactly why your research for that area applies using evidence from available sources. For example:

* DO NOT SAY: Oracle Corporation (ORCL) has likely benefitted from one-time compliance and licensing audits recently.
* SAY: Oracle Corporation (ORCL) has benefitted from one-time compliance and licensing audits based on `[REASON 1]`, `[REASON 2]`, and `[REASON 3]`, as highlighted in `[SOURCE(S)]`.

## General

1. Weakening moat.
   * Analyze the company's competitive moat and identify evidence suggesting that the moat may be weakening.
2. Consider the company's debt load.
   * Analyze the trajectory of the company's debt over available periods.
   * Analyze the company's debt load relative to competitors.
3. Consider short-term cyclical tailwinds that might soon transition to long-term headwinds.
   * If any of the company's business segments operate in a cyclical market, determine if the cycle may be at its peak in which case those segments are likely to under-perform in the near-term. 
4. Consider recent and expensive acquisitions.
   * Determine if the company is buying growth in order to mask organic weakness.
5. Weak business segment growth.
   * Consider if weak business segment growth signals a fundamental market or macroeconomic change.
6. Management refusal to raise guidance after a strong quarter.
   * If management refuses to raise guidance following a strong quarterly performance, consider if they may have benefitted from one or more short-term catalysts, but are worred about performance during the remaining fiscal period.

## Information Technology

1. **Outrageous Total Addressable Market ("TAM") claims.**
   * SpaceX (SPCX), as one example, suggested in their S-1 that they have a TAM of nearly $30 trillion. TAM claims are often impossible to verify due to their subjective nature. Flag and analyze outrageous TAM claims.
2. **Outrageous moonshot claims.**
   * Many information technology are involved in "moonshot" initiatives and have made claims about those initiatives. For example, IBM Corporation has claimed they will deliver a fault-tolerant quantum computer by 2029. This claim is disputed by leading physicists and other technology companies in the quantum computing space. Flag and analyze outrageous moonshot claims.
3. **Business segment manipulation.**
   * Most information technology companies will highlight one or more business segments as their growth segments. For example, when IBM Corporation bought Red Hat, their Hybrid Cloud business segment became one of closely-watched growth segments. A financial engineering trick that many companies use is to move legacy products into their growth segments each fiscal period to provide the appearance of growth.
4. **One-time compliance and licensing audits.**
   * Some software companies, especially those with complex licensing models, regularly audit their customers to ensure their use of licensed software is compliant with the terms of the license. Customers are often out-of-compliance due to not understanding their license terms, accidentally over-deploying software, and/or making other mistakes relative to their license terms. These customers are often "shaken-down" for large one-time fees to come back into compliance. This revenue can be significant in the quarter in which it is recognized, but is not durable as it reflects one-time transactions.
5. **Goodwill impairment.**
   * Most software companies buy growth - i.e. they make a lot of acquisitions. Most software acquisitions are arguably overpriced resulting in signficant goodwill on companies' balance sheets. Over time, accumulated goodwill may not reasonably reflect the true value of acquired assets as those assets decline in value - e.g. technologies become obsolete or commoditized, customer relationships erode, etc. Consider if a firm with significant goodwill might be impaired.

## Communication Services
1. **Subscriber churn manipulation and metric redefinition.**
   * Telecom, streaming, and interactive media companies live and die by user growth. Flag instances where management changes the definition of an "Active User" or "Subscriber" (e.g., bundling a struggling service for free with a popular one and counting it as a new paid subscriber) to mask deteriorating organic retention and rising churn rates.
2. **Aggressive capitalization and amortization of content costs.**
   * Media and entertainment companies spend heavily on content creation. Analyze their amortization schedules. A bear-case flag is when a company stretches the amortization period for content (claiming it has a longer "useful life") to artificially lower current-period expenses and inflate near-term earnings, delaying the inevitable write-downs.
3. **Over-reliance on low-quality, cyclical advertising revenue.**
   * For interactive media and social platforms, analyze the composition of ad spend. Determine if recent revenue growth was driven by highly cyclical, low-quality, or transient advertisers (e.g., crypto companies, dropshippers, or political ad spend) rather than durable enterprise brand advertising.

## Consumer Discretionary
1. **"Same-Store Sales" (Comparable Sales) engineering.**
   * Retailers and restaurants often manipulate "comps" to show growth. Analyze if the company is quietly closing underperforming stores (removing them from the metric) or changing the timeframe of what constitutes a "comparable" store to artificially boost the aggregate growth number, hiding declining foot traffic.
2. **Channel stuffing and inventory mark-down avoidance.**
   * Analyze inventory growth relative to sales growth. Flag if a company is aggressively shipping products to wholesale partners ("channel stuffing") to book revenue early, or refusing to take necessary impairment charges/markdowns on obsolete inventory, artificially inflating current gross margins.
3. **Relaxing credit standards in captive finance arms.**
   * Auto manufacturers and large retailers often have in-house financing divisions. Investigate if sales volume is being propped up by loosening credit standards, offering sub-prime loans, or artificially extending loan terms. This shifts the risk from the income statement (sales) to the balance sheet (future bad debt).

## Consumer Staples
1. **Price masking volume deterioration (Volume Elasticity breaking).**
   * Consumer staples companies often boast about "pricing power." Analyze the revenue mix between Price/Mix and Volume. A strong bear case emerges when revenue growth is driven entirely by aggressive price hikes while underlying unit volumes are steadily shrinking, signaling brand deterioration and an impending limit to consumer tolerance.
2. **Trade-spend and promotional misclassification.**
   * Staples companies pay heavy slotting fees and promotional discounts to retailers ("trade spend"). Scrutinize whether management is categorizing these as operating expenses rather than deductions from gross revenue, or capitalizing them, to artificially inflate net sales figures.
3. **Short-sighted SKU rationalization.**
   * To boost short-term margins, management may aggressively discontinue lower-margin products (SKU rationalization). While this briefly improves profitability, investigate if this has resulted in a permanent loss of retail shelf space to competitors, capping long-term revenue growth.

## Energy
1. **Aggressive Reserve Replacement and 1P manipulation.**
   * Oil & Gas valuations rely heavily on Proven (1P) reserves. Flag aggressive assumptions regarding extraction costs or technological feasibility used to reclassify lower-probability reserves into the 1P category. This artificially inflates the company's net asset value and hides a deteriorating reserve replacement ratio.
2. **Misclassifying OPEX as CAPEX.**
   * Energy companies have massive maintenance costs. Scrutinize the cash flow statement to see if the company is classifying routine maintenance and operating expenses (OPEX) as capital expenditures (CAPEX). This accounting trick artificially boosts current-period earnings and Cash Flow from Operations.
3. **Rolling off of favorable hedging contracts.**
   * Analyze the company's derivative and hedging portfolio. Determine if recent strong margins were purely the result of highly favorable, short-term hedges locked in during previous price spikes. If these contracts are rolling off, the company may be newly exposed to unhedged downside in a weak commodity price environment.

## Financials
1. **Premature release of Loan Loss Reserves.**
   * Banks and lenders establish reserves (Allowance for Credit Losses) to cover bad debt. A classic earnings-management trick is to release these reserves back into the income statement during quarters with weak operational performance, artificially boosting Earnings Per Share (EPS) while masking underlying weakness in the loan book.
2. **Hiding unrealized losses in Held-to-Maturity (HTM) portfolios.**
   * Scrutinize the balance sheet for massive transfers of depreciating assets from "Available-for-Sale" to "Held-to-Maturity." This accounting maneuver allows institutions to avoid marking assets to market, hiding deep unrealized losses that could threaten tier-1 capital ratios or equity.
3. **Aggressive actuarial assumptions in Insurance float.**
   * For insurance companies, current-period profitability is highly dependent on estimates of future payouts. Investigate if management has recently altered their actuarial assumptions (e.g., assuming lower inflation for medical costs or longer payout tails) to artificially lower claims reserves and boost current underwriting income.

## Health Care
1. **Overstating pipeline TAM to mask legacy patent cliffs.**
   * Pharmaceutical companies facing the loss of exclusivity (patent cliffs) on blockbuster drugs will often aggressively overstate the Total Addressable Market, probability of FDA approval, or peak sales estimates of their Phase II/III pipeline to distract investors from impending revenue destruction.
2. **Immediate write-offs of In-Process R&D (IPR&D).**
   * When healthcare companies make acquisitions, they often allocate a massive portion of the purchase price to "In-Process R&D" and immediately write it off as a one-time charge. This prevents them from having to amortize that cost over time, making future operating margins look artificially high.
3. **Opaque reliance on PBM rebates.**
   * Analyze gross-to-net revenue dynamics. A company might show strong gross revenue growth, but if that growth requires increasingly aggressive rebates and kickbacks to Pharmacy Benefit Managers (PBMs) to secure formulary placement, the company has no actual pricing power. 

## Industrials
1. **Aggressive Percentage-of-Completion accounting.**
   * Industrial companies with long-term contracts (e.g., aerospace, defense, construction) recognize revenue over time. Flag instances where management uses overly optimistic cost estimates or milestones to pull revenue forward into the current quarter long before cash is actually collected or the product is delivered.
2. **Backlog stuffing with "Zombie" orders.**
   * Industrial valuations are heavily tied to their order backlog. Investigate the quality of the backlog. Companies will often refuse to purge soft, non-binding orders, or orders from financially distressed customers ("zombie orders"), to mask a fundamental slowdown in new demand.
3. **Underfunding warranty reserves on new product lines.**
   * When introducing complex new machinery or equipment, management may intentionally underfund their warranty reserves to boost initial gross margins. This sets a trap: when the equipment inevitably experiences field failures, the company will be hit with massive, unexpected liabilities.

## Materials
1. **LIFO inventory liquidation.**
   * In inflationary environments, materials companies (metals, chemicals) using Last-In, First-Out (LIFO) accounting might intentionally dip into older, low-cost inventory layers. Selling this artificially cheap historical inventory generates a massive, unsustainable one-time boost to gross margins that cannot be repeated.
2. **Under-reserving for Asset Retirement Obligations (AROs).**
   * Mining and chemical companies face massive end-of-life cleanup and environmental liabilities. Scrutinize the discount rates and cost assumptions used for AROs. Artificially lowering these estimates boosts the current book value of equity and inflates earnings.
3. **Shifting debt and losses to unconsolidated Joint Ventures.**
   * Materials companies frequently operate globally through complex joint ventures. Investigate whether the parent company is using transfer pricing to shift losses, or keeping massive amounts of project debt off the primary balance sheet through unconsolidated VIEs (Variable Interest Entities).

## Real Estate
1. **AFFO manipulation via CAPEX add-backs.**
   * Real Estate Investment Trusts (REITs) are valued on Adjusted Funds From Operations (AFFO). Because AFFO is a non-GAAP metric, management has discretion over its calculation. A severe red flag is management aggressively adding back recurring, necessary maintenance CAPEX to artificially inflate AFFO, making the dividend look safer than it actually is.
2. **Unrealistic Capitalization Rates (Cap Rates) driving NAV.**
   * Scrutinize the Cap Rates management uses to value their property portfolio. In a rising interest rate environment, using historically low Cap Rates artificially inflates the Net Asset Value (NAV) of the properties, hiding potential breaches of Loan-to-Value (LTV) debt covenants.
3. **Excessive rent concessions masking falling economic occupancy.**
   * To keep "headline" rent metrics looking strong, property managers will often offer massive, unpublicized upfront concessions (e.g., 6 months of free rent or massive Tenant Improvement allowances). This masks a deterioration in true economic occupancy and effective rents.

## Utilities
1. **Capitalizing operating failures as "Regulatory Assets."**
   * Utilities operate in highly regulated environments. A massive red flag is a utility capitalizing the costs of their own operational failures, lawsuits, or extreme weather events as "Regulatory Assets," assuming they can just pass these costs onto ratepayers later. If the utility commission denies the rate hike, these assets turn into catastrophic write-downs.
2. **Funding the dividend through debt or equity dilution.**
   * Utilities are owned primarily for their steady dividends. Analyze the cash flow statement to ensure Operating Cash Flow comfortably covers both CAPEX and the dividend. If the utility is routinely issuing new debt or diluting equity to cover the dividend payout, the yield is fundamentally unsupported and at risk of being cut.
3. **The "Tax Cliff" from Deferred Tax Liabilities.**
   * Utilities often use bonus depreciation and complex tax maneuvers to push tax payments into the future, creating massive Deferred Tax Liabilities (DTLs) which temporarily boost cash flow. Flag companies where these DTLs are nearing reversal; when they do, the company will face a severe "tax cliff" that will suddenly drain operating cash flow.
